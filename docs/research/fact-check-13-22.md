## Week 13 — Bald Eagle egg

### Fact 13.1
> A Bald Eagle nest can reach two meters across and weigh a tonne after years of additions.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**
> The nest is the largest of any bird in North America; it is used repeatedly over many years and with new material added each year may eventually be as large as 4 m (13 ft) deep, 2.5 m (8.2 ft) across and weigh 1 metric ton (1.1 short tons). One nest in Florida was found to be 6.1 m (20 ft) deep, 2.9 meters (9.5 ft) across, and to weigh 3 short tons (2.7 metric tons). This nest is on record as the largest tree nest ever recorded for any animal.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bald_eagle (Wikipedia)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable (403). Wikipedia (opened) supports the claim as written.

**Note:** This was flagged as the likeliest-wrong fact in the range, specifically for conflating a *record* nest with a *typical* one. Having read it closely: the fact's own wording ("can reach... after years of additions") already matches Wikipedia's framing of an accumulated, long-reused nest, not a typical first-built nest and not the single all-time record. The record (Florida, 1963) is meaningfully bigger — 2.9 m across and ~2.7–3 metric tons, almost 3x the weight this fact states. A typical nest, per multiple web sources (Birdfact, American Eagle Foundation, etc.), is closer to 1.5–1.8 m across and much lighter — well under a tonne. So "two meters, a tonne" is neither typical nor the record; it's the "may eventually be" figure for a nest reused over many years, which is what the fact says. Leave as is, but a human should not tighten the wording to imply this is either "typical" or "the record" — it's neither.

**Verdict:** Partly supported

**Evidence:**

### Fact 21.2
> Kestrels see ultraviolet, which makes the urine trails of voles show up along the ground.

**Verdict:** Contradicted

**Evidence:**
The cited Wikipedia article never makes this claim — a full-text fetch of `en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_kestrel` (31,035 characters) contains zero occurrences of "ultraviolet" and zero of "urine." The underlying research this claim is popularly traced to is real, but it was never about this species:

> Viitala, J., E. Korpimäki, P. Palokangas, and M. Koivula, "Attraction of Kestrels to vole scent marks visible in ultraviolet light," Nature 373:425–427 (1995) — a field study of the **common/Eurasian kestrel, *Falco tinnunculus*, in Finland**, not the American kestrel (*Falco sparverius*) that this app's week 21 is about.

And the hypothesis itself has since been directly tested and rejected, again in the Eurasian kestrel and other Old World raptors:

> "It seems unlikely that vole urine can provide a reliable visual cue under natural circumstances where weak signals are confounded by urine from other mammals, destroyed by rain and wind, and seen from a distance where the low spatial resolution of chromatic vision has to be considered." … "Raptors do not use UV cues for detection in these interactions, and our data suggest that it is unlikely that bank vole and field vole urine provide any visual cue that raptors can use."
> — Lind, O. et al., "Ultraviolet sensitivity and colour vision in raptor foraging," *J. Exp. Biol.* 216:1819–1826 (2013); species tested: common buzzard, sparrowhawk, red kite, and kestrel (*Falco tinnunculus*).

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_kestrel (Wikipedia, fetched live in full, via the action API — confirms the claim is absent); https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/216/10/1819/11547 (Lind et al. 2013, J. Exp. Biol., fetched live); https://www.nature.com/articles/373425a0 (Viitala et al. 1995, identified via search, species confirmed via secondary sources)

**Cited source status:** Cited to Wikipedia, which was fetched in full and does not contain this claim anywhere — the citation is simply wrong for this fact, independent of the species issue.

**Note:** This needs a rewrite, not a citation swap. Two separate problems stack here: (1) species drift — the only research behind this claim studied the Eurasian kestrel, not the American kestrel this fact is about, and nothing in this pass found the UV/vole-urine effect demonstrated in *Falco sparverius*; (2) the finding is contested even for the species it was studied in — a 2013 follow-up using direct measurement of raptor ocular transmittance concluded UV vole-urine detection is "unlikely." What does survive: American kestrels, like many birds, likely have some UV sensitivity (true broadly of falcons), but the specific "sees vole urine trails via UV" mechanism should not be stated as settled fact for this species. Recommend dropping the fact or replacing it with something the corpus actually supports (e.g., the hovering/kiting behavior already covered in 21.3, or its cavity-nesting habits).
