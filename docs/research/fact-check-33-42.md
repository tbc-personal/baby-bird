## Week 33 — Green Heron

### Fact 33.1
> Green Herons drop insects, feathers or bread on the water as bait and wait for a fish to rise to it.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**
> Green herons have been observed using captured prey (e.g. mayflies) or other objects (bread, feathers) to "bait-fish", using a lure on the water's surface to attract fish. This bait-fishing behavior is common among herons.

> In particular, they commonly use bread crusts, insects, or other items as bait. The bait is dropped onto the surface of a body of water to lure fish.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_heron (Wikipedia)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. Wikipedia (opened) supports the claim as written, including all three named bait types.

**Note:** Solid, leave as is.

### Fact 33.2
> They hunt with the neck folded back and strike from it, which makes them look far shorter than they are.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**
> The neck is often pulled in tight against the body.

> A Green Heron's split-second extension from apparent stumpiness is an especially impressive sight to behold. […] when a heron hunts with its neck fully retracted against its body, it creates an element of surprise. The retracted position makes prey perceive the bird as farther away than it actually is, allowing the heron to suddenly extend its neck and strike with speed.

**Source:** https://www.audubon.org/magazine/more-you-ever-wanted-know-about-heron-necks (Audubon Magazine, fetched live); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_heron (Wikipedia)

**Cited source status:** Cited to Wikipedia, which was opened but only gives the general "neck pulled in tight" description, not the hunting-strike behavior or the "looks shorter" effect. Audubon's magazine (not the field guide) is the better match and was needed to confirm the specific mechanism.

**Note:** Accurate. Swap or add the Audubon magazine piece as citation — Wikipedia alone under-supports this one.

### Fact 33.3
> The eggs are pale green-blue, laid on a loose stick platform over water.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**

> The nest is a platform of twigs and sticks lined with finer material. Some nests are so shallow and flimsy that the eggs can be seen through the bottom. […] Green herons usually nest in shrubs or trees overhanging the water.

**Source:** https://www.audubon.org/field-guide/bird/green-heron (Audubon Field Guide, opened); https://www.pa.gov/agencies/pgc/wildlife/discover-pa-wildlife/herons/green-heron (PA Game Commission, fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. Audubon Field Guide (opened) confirms egg color; PA Game Commission confirms "loose"/flimsy platform over water.

**Note:** Solid, leave as is.

## Week 34 — Ring-billed Gull

### Fact 34.1
> Ring-billed Gulls come back to the colony where they hatched, often to within a few meters of the spot.

**Verdict:** Partly supported

**Evidence:**
> In one study, 69% of banded ring-billed gulls returned to their natal colony to nest, and 90% returned to a colony where they had nested previously.

**Source:** https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Larus_delawarensis/ (Animal Diversity Web, fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. Wikipedia (opened, the only other cited/candidate source) doesn't mention philopatry at all — its Ring-billed Gull article has no breeding-behavior section. ADW, fetched live, is the only source read that speaks to this.

**Note:** The colony-level return (69%, i.e. "the colony where they hatched") is solid. The "within a few meters of the spot" detail could not be confirmed for *natal* return specifically — the "few meters" figure that turns up in secondary sources (Birds of the World's paywalled demography page, echoed in search snippets as ~20% within 3 m) describes an *adult's own* nest-site fidelity across breeding years, a related but distinct phenomenon from a chick returning near its hatching spot. A human should soften this to something like "often to the same colony, sometimes within meters of their own previous nest" rather than tying the meters figure to the hatching location.

### Fact 34.2
> Chicks peck at a parent's bill to make it bring food back up for them.

**Verdict:** Partly supported / mechanism contradicted for this species

**Evidence:**
> In a series of field experiments, adult Ring-billed Gulls attending either blindfolded or surgically muted chicks were observed in order to ascertain whether various patterns of parental behavior would be adversely affected by the failure of the chicks to peck the parent's bill or to vocalize. […] Adults attending chicks that had been muted at hatching showed normal attentive and protective behavior but were markedly deficient in providing the chicks with food. […] the bill-pecking of chicks apparently is neither essential nor sufficient to incite normal parental food-provisioning behavior. On the other hand, this provisioning behavior does appear to depend temporarily on auditory reception of the vocalizations of chicks located in the nest.

**Source:** Miller, D. E. & Conover, M. R. (1979), "Differential Effects of Chick Vocalizations and Bill-Pecking on Parental Behavior in the Ring-Billed Gull," *The Auk* 96(2):284, https://academic.oup.com/auk/article-abstract/96/2/284/5184810 (fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to Wikipedia, which was opened but says nothing about chick begging, bill color, or feeding mechanics — its Ring-billed Gull article has no breeding-behavior content at all.

**Note:** This is one of the three claims flagged as most likely wrong, and the check confirms the concern in a specific way. The classic red-spot begging story (Tinbergen & Perdeck) is about **Herring Gull**, not Ring-billed Gull, and Ring-billed Gull's bill has a black ring, not a red gonys spot (confirmed in this week's own Wikipedia/Audubon corpus: "yellow bill with a black ring"). Bill-pecking by chicks *is* documented behavior in Ring-billed Gull specifically — but the only species-specific study found (Miller & Conover 1979) shows the pecking does **not** cause the parent to regurgitate; muted chicks that still pecked normally got markedly less food, while vocalizations are what the paper found actually triggers provisioning. So the fact has the right species and the right general behavior (chicks do peck) but the wrong causal mechanism — it isn't the peck that "makes" the parent feed them, per the one paper that tested this in Ring-billed Gulls. Rewrite to either drop the causal claim ("chicks peck at the parent's bill while begging") or reframe around vocalization as the trigger.

### Fact 34.3
> They nest on the ground in dense island colonies, out of reach of most mammals.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**

> They nest on the ground in colonies on sparsely vegetated islands in large lakes, and occasionally on mainland peninsulas and on near-shore oceanic islands.

> Gulls do not normally have many natural predators; nesting on islands or in marshes safeguards their nests from most land predators.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring-billed_gull (Wikipedia); https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/ribgul/cur/introduction (Birds of the World); https://www.hww.ca/wildlife/birds/ring-billed-gull/ (Hinterland Who's Who, fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. Wikipedia and BOW (both opened) plus Hinterland Who's Who (fetched live) all corroborate.

**Note:** Solid, leave as is.

**Note:** Solid, leave as is.

### Fact 35.2
> Pairs hold the same territory for years and add to the same stick nest each spring.

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**
> The same nesting territory may be defended by the pair for years.

> The same nest may be renovated and used again for several years. A nest that has been refurbished multiple times is usually larger than average. […] Reused nests are always cleaned and renovated before the eggs are laid.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red-tailed_hawk (Wikipedia, cited source); https://www.birdfact.com/articles/red-tailed-hawk-nesting (birdfact.com, fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to Wikipedia, which was opened and directly supports the territory-fidelity half; the nest-reuse-and-enlargement half needed a supplementary live source, since Wikipedia's own corpus text here actually emphasizes that red-tails often build a *new* nest nearby when an old one is lost to owls, rather than always reusing one nest.

**Note:** Broadly accurate but slightly oversimplified — pairs often maintain a few alternate nests within a territory and pick among them each spring rather than mechanically adding to one single nest forever. Fine to leave, but a human could soften "the same stick nest" to "a nest in the same territory."

**Verdict:** Supported

**Evidence:**

> The male assists in the nesting period, doing some of the incubation but spending most of his time feeding the female while she incubates or feeds chicks.

**Source:** https://birdsoftheworld.org/bow/species/rethaw/cur/introduction (Birds of the World, opened); Vermont Fish & Wildlife Department, https://www.vtfishandwildlife.com/learn-more/vermont-critters/birds/red-tailed-hawk (fetched live)

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. BOW (opened) gives the clutch size; Wikipedia's own corpus gives a wider "one to five" range, so "two or three" reflects BOW's "typical" framing rather than the absolute range. Vermont F&W confirms egg color and the female's larger incubation share.

**Note:** Solid, leave as is; egg count varies a bit by source (1–5 absolute range) but "two or three" matches the commonly cited typical clutch.

## Week 36 — Wood Duck

**Note:** Solid, leave as is.

### Fact 40.2
> Clutch size tracks the lemming supply, from three eggs in a poor year to eleven in a good one.

**Verdict:** Partly supported

**Evidence:**
> The snowy owl lays a very large clutch of eggs, often from about 5 to 11, with the laying and hatching of eggs considerably staggered.

> The clutch is extremely variable in size averaging around 7–9, with up to 15 or 16 eggs recorded in extreme cases. […] Mean clutch sizes were 7.5 in a sample of 24 in Hooper Bay (range of 5–11); 6.7 in a sample of seven from Utqiaġvik (4–9) […]

> The Utqiaġvik nests bore three to ten sized-clutches with a mean of six eggs per nest.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snowy_owl (Wikipedia, cited source)

**Cited source status:** Direct match to the cited source, which is the source of both the general mechanism and the specific numbers, though not as one clean range.

**Note:** This was flagged as a from-memory range worth checking on both ends. The mechanism (clutch size tracks food/lemming supply, and it's "extremely variable") is well supported. The *specific* "3 to 11" doesn't come from one unified statement, though: 3 is the low end recorded at one site (Utqiaġvik, "three to ten"), and 11 is the high end of a different, general overview line ("often from about 5 to 11"). The genuinely extreme high end the article records is 15–16 eggs, well above 11 — so if anything "eleven" understates the documented maximum rather than overstating it. A human should either cite this as an illustrative "typical variation" range (which it reasonably is) rather than hard floor/ceiling, or bump the top end toward the 15–16 extreme if precision is wanted.

**Cited source status:** Cited to All About Birds, unopenable. BOW (opened) gives the talon strength; NY DEC and a Naturally Curious (Mary Holland) piece, both fetched live, confirm skunks are otherwise avoided by predators (bobcats, foxes, coyotes only take them when desperate).

**Note:** True as stated, but the causal link is slightly loose: what actually sets the great horned owl apart from other skunk-capable predators is its poor sense of smell, not its grip strength — foxes and coyotes are physically capable of killing a skunk too, they just won't. Fine to leave; a human could tighten by noting the smell angle instead of grip strength.

## Needs a human decision

- **Fact 34.2** (Ring-billed Gull bill-pecking): the fact is defensible as
  "chicks peck at the parent's bill" (documented for this species) but wrong
  as written that pecking is what "make[s]" the parent regurgitate — the one
  study that tested this in Ring-billed Gull found vocalizations do that, not
  pecking. Needs a rewrite decision, not just a citation swap.
- **Fact 40.2** (Snowy Owl clutch range): "3 to 11" is defensible as an
  illustrative range but doesn't match any single cited figure exactly, and
  undersells the documented extreme maximum (15–16). Decide whether to keep
  it as a loose illustrative range, tighten it to the article's own "5 to
  11" overview line, or widen the top end.
- **Fact 34.1** (Ring-billed Gull natal return distance): the "few meters of
  the spot" detail could not be traced to a source about natal return
  specifically (as opposed to adult nest-site fidelity across years) within
  what's reachable from here. If a human has access to the paywalled Birds
  of the World demography page, it may resolve this cleanly one way or the
  other.
- **Fact 35.2** (Red-tailed Hawk "the same stick nest"): true in the common
  case but Wikipedia's own text on this species emphasizes that a *new*
  nest is often built nearby when the old one is lost — worth a light
  rewrite ("a nest in the same territory") if precision matters more than
  brevity.
